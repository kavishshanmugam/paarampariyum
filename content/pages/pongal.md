# Pongal

## In two sentences

A four-day Tamil harvest thanksgiving when freshly harvested rice is cooked in milk until the pot overflows -- and everyone shouts "Pongalo Pongal!" It is dedicated to Surya (the sun), to cattle, and to the earth that feeds us.

## In Melbourne

The engine computes the sun's entry into sidereal Capricorn for Melbourne. Thai Pongal typically falls on 14 or 15 January -- high summer in Melbourne, which actually matches the harvest spirit well. Melbourne's Tamil community runs large Pongal events in parks and community halls.

## The story

**Short version.** "Pongal" means "to boil over." Freshly harvested rice is cooked in new milk until it overflows the pot, symbolising abundance. The festival thanks Surya for the harvest, honours cattle for their labour, and celebrates family.

**Full version.** Pongal is one of the oldest continuously observed festivals in the subcontinent, with references in Sangam literature (circa 200 BCE - 300 CE). The Perumpanatruppadai and Maduraikanchi describe harvest celebrations with rice-boiling rituals. Unlike many Hindu festivals, Pongal's roots are agrarian rather than Puranic -- it predates the Puranic literature.

The four days each carry meaning:

**Bhogi** (Day 1): Dedicated to Indra, lord of rain. Old and broken items are burned in a bonfire before dawn -- a ritual purging of the past year. The Bhagavata Purana (Skandha 10) connects this to the tension between Indra-worship and Krishna-worship: Krishna told the people of Vrindavan to worship the harvest itself, not the rain-giver.

**Thai Pongal / Surya Pongal** (Day 2, the main day): The rice-in-milk ritual. The overflowing pot is the central act. Offered to Surya facing east.

**Mattu Pongal** (Day 3): Honours cattle -- specifically Nandi, Shiva's bull. The Periya Puranam recounts that Shiva sent Nandi to tell humans to eat once a day. Nandi got confused and said "eat three times a day." Shiva said, "Fine -- then go help them grow enough food." This is why cattle work the fields and are honoured on this day.

**Kaanum Pongal** (Day 4): Family day. Sisters pray for their brothers' wellbeing. Families visit parks, rivers, and relatives. Leftover Pongal rice is placed on turmeric leaves for birds and animals.

*Sources: Sangam literature (Perumpanatruppadai, Maduraikanchi), Bhagavata Purana (Skandha 10), Periya Puranam, Tirukkural (Chapter 104)*

## What to do

### Ten-minute version

1. Cook rice in milk in a pot until it boils over -- let it overflow (this IS the ritual)
2. As it boils over, shout "Pongalo Pongal!"
3. Add jaggery, cardamom, cashews, and ghee
4. Offer the first portion to Surya facing east, outdoors if possible
5. Share and eat together as a family

### Fuller version

**Bhogi (Day 1):**
1. Before dawn, gather old or broken items from the house and burn them in a bonfire (or symbolically discard them)
2. Clean the house thoroughly
3. Draw a fresh kolam (rice flour rangoli) at the entrance -- elaborate, not the everyday one
4. In the evening, the Bhogi Pallu ceremony: shower small children with seasonal fruits and jujube berries

**Thai Pongal (Day 2 -- main day):**
1. Wake early; bathe and wear new clothes
2. Set up the Pongal pot outdoors in the courtyard or balcony, facing east
3. Use a new or freshly cleaned pot; tie a fresh turmeric plant and ginger to it
4. Boil fresh milk first, then add newly harvested rice
5. When it boils over -- "Pongalo Pongal!" -- this is the moment
6. Add jaggery (vellam), ghee, cardamom, cashews, raisins
7. Offer to Surya with turmeric, sugarcane, and bananas
8. Draw the largest, most elaborate kolam of the year
9. Cook Ven Pongal (savoury) and Sakkarai Pongal (sweet), vadai, payasam
10. Visit the temple; share food with neighbours

**Mattu Pongal (Day 3):**
1. Bathe and decorate cattle (or pet animals, in the diaspora) -- garland them, paint their horns, tie bells
2. Feed cattle Pongal rice, bananas, and jaggery
3. In rural Tamil Nadu, Jallikattu (bull-embracing sport) happens on this day

**Kaanum Pongal (Day 4):**
1. Family outing day -- visit relatives, go to parks or the beach
2. Sisters pray for the wellbeing of brothers
3. Place leftover Pongal rice on a turmeric leaf outside for birds and animals

### Items needed

- New clay or steel pot (dedicated for Pongal)
- Raw rice (ideally new-harvest rice, but any white rice works)
- Whole milk
- Jaggery (vellam) -- the brown block kind, not refined sugar
- Cardamom, cashew nuts, ghee, raisins
- Turmeric root or plant (tied to pot), fresh ginger
- Sugarcane stalks (3-5)
- Bananas, seasonal fruits
- Rice flour for kolam
- Turmeric leaves (for serving)
- New clothes

### In Melbourne

Indian and Sri Lankan grocers in Dandenong, Footscray, and Glen Waverley stock jaggery, rice flour, and sugarcane (in season around January). Sugarcane is the hardest item -- some South Indian grocers bring it in specifically for Pongal; order in advance from Sri Lankan or Tamil stores. Fresh turmeric root is at most Asian grocers. The Tamil associations in Melbourne (SBS Tamil radio community, Tamil Sangams) organise community Pongal events in January, often in parks with outdoor cooking.

## Why

**Scriptural.** Pongal is pre-Puranic and agrarian. Its authority comes from Tamil Sangam literature and over 2000 years of continuous practice. The Tirukkural (Chapter 104) speaks of gratitude to the earth and rain -- Pongal is the embodiment of this.

**Symbolic.** The boiling over of the pot = abundance overflowing. Jaggery (unrefined) rather than sugar = sweetness from the earth without excessive refinement. Sugarcane = straightforwardness. The kolam drawn with rice flour = an offering to ants and small creatures; even the decoration feeds something.

**Seasonal.** In the Tamil calendar, Margazhi (Dec-Jan) is considered inauspicious for new ventures; Thai (Jan-Feb) marks the return of auspiciousness. The sun moves northward (Uttarayana) and days begin to lengthen. In Melbourne, January is high summer -- the harvest spirit matches naturally.

## How others do it

- **Andhra Pradesh / Telangana:** Called Sankranti; 3 days: Bhogi, Sankranti, Kanuma. Haridasu (travelling bards) go door to door. Rangoli competitions are massive.
- **Karnataka:** Called Sankranti or Ellu Birodha. Exchange of sesame-jaggery mix (ellu-bella) with "Ellu bella thindu, olle maathu aadu" (eat sesame and jaggery, speak sweet words). Kite flying.
- **North India:** Makar Sankranti -- tilgul sweets, kite flying (Gujarat, Rajasthan), khichdi charity (UP/Bihar). Very different in character from the Tamil celebration.
- **Punjab:** Lohri the night before -- bonfire, popcorn, rewri, folk songs.

## Who is traditionally exempt

No fasting is associated with Pongal. It is entirely a festival of abundance and eating. No one is exempt because there is nothing to be exempted from. Those who are unwell simply participate as they can.
