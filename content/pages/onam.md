# Onam

## In two sentences

Kerala's great harvest festival, celebrating the annual return of the mythical King Mahabali -- an Asura king so just that his kingdom knew no poverty, no crime, no inequality. For ten days, homes are decorated with flower carpets (Pookalam), and the culmination is the grand vegetarian feast (Onam Sadya) served on a banana leaf.

## In Melbourne

Thiruvonam falls in August-September, which is late winter turning to spring in Melbourne. The Malayalee Association of Victoria (MAVA) organises annual Onam celebrations with a full Sadya, cultural programmes, and community events. Banana leaves are available year-round at Vietnamese and Sri Lankan grocers in Footscray and Springvale.

## The story

**Short version.** Mahabali was an Asura king so righteous that his kingdom was a golden age. The Devas grew jealous. Vishnu took the form of Vamana (a dwarf Brahmin) and tricked Mahabali into giving away his entire kingdom in three steps. Mahabali, true to his word, offered his own head for the third step. Vishnu, impressed by his virtue, granted him one boon: to return and visit his people once a year. That visit is Onam.

**Full version.** The Bhagavata Purana (Skandha 8, Chapters 15-23) gives the full account. Mahabali was the grandson of Prahlada. Through penance and dharmic rule, he had conquered the three worlds. The Devas approached Vishnu for help. Vishnu incarnated as Vamana, a small Brahmin boy, and appeared at Mahabali's great yajna. He asked for just three paces of land. Mahabali's guru Shukracharya warned him it was a trap, but Mahabali refused to break his word. Vamana grew to cosmic proportions: one step covered the earth, the second the heavens. For the third, Mahabali offered his head. Vamana pushed him to Sutala.

The key point: Vishnu did not punish Mahabali for being evil -- he was entirely virtuous. Vishnu honoured his sacrifice by making him king of Sutala (a pleasant realm), by serving as his personal doorkeeper, and by granting the annual return.

The famous Onam song begins: "Maaveli naadu vaanidum kaalam / Manushyarellarum onnu pole" -- "When Maveli ruled the land, all people were equal."

*Sources: Bhagavata Purana (Skandha 8), Vishnu Purana (Book 5), Matsya Purana*

## What to do

### Ten-minute version

1. Draw a Pookalam (flower rangoli) at the entrance using fresh flower petals
2. Prepare or order a simple Onam Sadya -- rice with sambar, avial, and payasam on a banana leaf
3. Eat together as a family, seated on the floor if possible
4. Place a small clay figure of Mahabali (Onathappan) at the centre of the Pookalam

### Fuller version

**The 10-day celebration (Atham to Thiruvonam):**
- **Atham (Day 1):** Begin the Pookalam with a small circle of flowers. Add a new ring each day.
- **Days 2-9:** The Pookalam grows larger and more elaborate daily. Traditional flowers: thumba (leucas), mukkutti (bidens), chembarathi (hibiscus), chethi (ixora). In Melbourne, use whatever seasonal flowers are available -- roses, marigolds, chrysanthemums, daisies.
- **Uthradam (Day 9):** "First Onam." Major shopping and preparation day. Clean the house. Prepare for the Sadya.

**Thiruvonam (Day 10 -- main day):**

**Morning:**
1. Wake early; bathe and wear new clothes (the Kerala kasavu -- cream/white with gold border -- is traditional)
2. Complete the final, most elaborate Pookalam
3. Place the Onathappan (a conical clay figure representing Mahabali) at the Pookalam's centre
4. Light a nilavilakku (traditional Kerala brass lamp)
5. Offer flowers, rice, and coconut

**The Onam Sadya:**
Served on a banana leaf. Traditionally 26 items, but even a simpler version should include:
- Rice (matta/red rice or white)
- Sambar, Rasam, Parippu curry (dal with ghee)
- Avial (mixed vegetables in coconut-yogurt -- said to have been invented by Bhima)
- Olan (ash gourd in coconut milk)
- Thoran (dry vegetable stir-fry with coconut)
- Pachadi and Kichadi (yogurt condiments)
- Pickles: Inji puli (ginger tamarind), mango pickle
- Papadum, Banana chips (upperi, fried in coconut oil)
- Payasam (at least 2: Palada pradhaman, Parippu pradhaman)
- Banana, Ghee

The Sadya is strictly vegetarian, even for families that eat meat on other days.

**Afternoon/Evening:**
- Cultural programmes, Thiruvathira Kali (women's circle dance), Pulikali (tiger dance)
- Visit family elders; give Onakkodi (new clothes) to family members

### Items needed

- Fresh flowers for Pookalam (any seasonal flowers)
- Banana leaves (available at Asian/Sri Lankan grocers)
- Coconut oil (essential -- Kerala cooking is defined by coconut oil)
- Sadya ingredients (see list above)
- Kerala kasavu clothing (optional but meaningful)
- Onathappan clay figure (often handmade)
- Nilavilakku (Kerala brass lamp)

### In Melbourne

Banana leaves are available year-round at Vietnamese/Sri Lankan grocers in Footscray and Springvale. Coconut oil and Kerala spices are stocked at most Indian grocers. The Malayalee Association of Victoria (MAVA) organises the main community Onam in Melbourne with a full Sadya and cultural programmes in August-September.

## Why

**Scriptural.** The Vamana avatar story is one of the Dashavatara (ten incarnations of Vishnu). Mahabali's generosity, even at the cost of his kingdom, is held as the highest form of dana (giving).

**Symbolic.** The Sadya on a banana leaf is deliberately egalitarian: everyone eats the same food, in the same way, seated on the ground. There is no hierarchy at the Sadya. This reflects the ideal of Mahabali's kingdom where "all people were equal."

**Seasonal.** In Kerala, Chingam marks the end of the monsoon and the beginning of the harvest. In Melbourne, August-September is late winter turning to spring -- a time of renewal.

## How others do it

- **Kerala:** Onam is the state festival, celebrated by Hindus, Christians, and Muslims alike as a cultural event
- **Tulu Nadu (coastal Karnataka):** Similar harvest traditions under different names
- **Diaspora:** Community Sadyas in hired halls are the standard celebration format

## Who is traditionally exempt

Onam has no fast or restriction. The Sadya is vegetarian but that is tradition, not hardship. Recently bereaved families may observe a quieter Onam. There is nothing punitive about the observance.
