# Bhogi Pongal

## In two sentences

Bhogi is the first day of the four-day Pongal festival, dedicated to Indra, the lord of rain and clouds. It is a day of purging the old -- families rise before dawn to burn worn-out belongings in a bonfire, clean the house, and draw elaborate kolams to welcome the harvest season.

## In Melbourne

Bhogi falls the day before Thai Pongal, typically 13 or 14 January -- a Melbourne summer day. While a full street bonfire is not practical in suburban Melbourne, families mark the spirit by decluttering, discarding old items, and deep-cleaning the house. Some Tamil community groups organise symbolic Bhogi fires at park events (with council permits). The elaborate kolam-drawing tradition is very much alive -- families draw large kolams on driveways and porches in the morning.

## The story

**Short version.** "Bhogi" refers to Indra, also called Bhogi (the enjoyer). The day is about releasing the past year's accumulation -- physically and symbolically -- through fire. Old clothes, broken furniture, dried leaves, and worn mats are burned in a pre-dawn bonfire. The house is swept clean, and the threshold is decorated with the year's most elaborate kolam.

**Full version.** Bhogi has deep roots in both agrarian practice and Puranic tradition. The Bhagavata Purana (Skandha 10) tells of Krishna advising the people of Vrindavan to honour the harvest and Govardhana hill rather than performing the traditional Indra puja. Indra, enraged, sent torrential rains, which Krishna lifted Govardhana hill to shield against. Eventually Indra accepted that gratitude belongs to the earth and the harvest, not just the rain-giver. Despite this, Bhogi retains Indra's association -- an acknowledgement that rain made the harvest possible.

The Bhogi bonfire (Bhogi Mantalu in Telugu regions) is both practical and ritual. After the cool Margazhi month, the fire warms the neighbourhood before dawn. Burning old things is a tangible act of letting go -- the Tamil concept of "pazhaiyana kazhithal, pudhiyana puguthal" (out with the old, in with the new).

The kolam tradition on Bhogi is especially significant. In Tamil Nadu, Bhogi is when kolam competitions happen -- streets and neighbourhoods compete for the most intricate designs. Kolams are drawn with rice flour, which feeds ants and insects -- even the art is an offering to other creatures.

The Bhogi Pallu ceremony is performed for young children: seasonal fruits, sugarcane pieces, jujube berries (regi pandlu / elanthai), coins, and flowers are showered over children while elders recite blessings. This is believed to ward off the evil eye and bless the child with abundance.

*Sources: Bhagavata Purana (Skandha 10), Sangam literature references to harvest-fire customs, Tamil folk tradition*

## What to do

### Ten-minute version

1. Gather old or broken items from around the house and discard or donate them
2. Clean the house entrance thoroughly
3. Draw a fresh, elaborate kolam at the front entrance with rice flour
4. Light a small lamp (or candle) at dawn facing east to honour Indra and the coming harvest

### Fuller version

**Before dawn:**
1. Rise early, before sunrise
2. Gather old clothes, broken items, dried branches, and worn-out household goods
3. Build a small bonfire (Bhogi Mantalu) and burn them -- in Melbourne, a fire pit in the backyard works, or simply bag items for donation/disposal as a symbolic release
4. As the fire burns, the family gathers around it, marking the end of the old year's clutter

**Morning:**
1. Deep-clean the entire house -- sweep, mop, dust
2. Draw an elaborate kolam at the front entrance with rice flour (this should be grander than the everyday kolam)
3. Decorate the entrance with mango leaves (toran) and fresh flowers
4. Bathe and wear clean (not necessarily new -- save new clothes for Thai Pongal)

**Bhogi Pallu ceremony (for families with young children):**
1. Seat the child on a low stool or mat
2. Shower them with jujube berries (elanthai/regi pandlu), seasonal fruits, flowers, coins, and sugarcane pieces
3. Elders recite blessings for health and prosperity
4. This is believed to remove drishti (evil eye) from the child

**Evening:**
1. Prepare simple festive food -- not the Pongal rice (that is for tomorrow), but sweets and savouries
2. Visit neighbours, admire each other's kolams
3. Prepare items for tomorrow's Thai Pongal cooking

### Items needed

- Old items to discard or burn (symbolic purging)
- Rice flour for kolam
- Mango leaves and flowers for entrance decoration
- For Bhogi Pallu: jujube berries (elanthai), seasonal fruits, flowers, coins, sugarcane pieces
- Lamp or vilakku (brass oil lamp)
- Cleaning supplies for the deep clean
- Fire pit or chiminea (if doing the bonfire in Melbourne)

### In Melbourne

Jujube berries (elanthai) can be hard to find -- try Sri Lankan and Tamil grocers in Dandenong or Footscray around Pongal season. Some families substitute with dried berries or dates. Mango leaves for the toran are available at South Indian grocers. For the bonfire, check your local council's fire regulations -- many Melbourne suburbs allow contained fire pits in backyards during non-total-fire-ban days. January is fire season, so check the CFA website for any total fire ban declarations before lighting any fire outdoors.

## Why

**Scriptural.** Bhogi honours Indra as the provider of rain that made the harvest possible. The Bhagavata Purana story of Krishna and Indra teaches that both the rain and the harvest deserve gratitude -- neither alone is sufficient.

**Symbolic.** The bonfire is release -- burning the old to make space for the new. The kolam drawn with rice flour feeds the smallest creatures even as it beautifies the threshold. The Bhogi Pallu showering of children with fruits and berries is a tactile blessing -- abundance literally poured over the next generation.

**Seasonal.** Bhogi falls at the transition from Margazhi (considered inauspicious for new ventures) to Thai (the return of auspiciousness). The cleaning and purging prepare the household -- physically and spiritually -- for the new season. In Melbourne's January summer, the impulse to declutter and refresh aligns naturally.

## Prayers and Mantras

**Indra Gayatri**
ஓம் சக்ராய வித்மஹே வஜ்ரஹஸ்தாய தீமஹி | தன்னோ இந்த்ரஃ ப்ரசோதயாத் ||
*Om Sahasrakshaya Vidmahe Vajrahastaya Dheemahi | Tanno Indrah Prachodayat*
"We meditate on the thousand-eyed one who holds the thunderbolt; may Indra illuminate our path."
The Gayatri addressed to Indra, appropriate for Bhogi as the day dedicated to the lord of rain.

**Bhogi Invocation (Tamil folk prayer)**
போகி புண்ணியம் வருக, பழையன கழிக, புதியன புகுக!
*Bhogi punniyam varuga, pazhaiyana kazhiga, pudhiyana puguga!*
"May the merit of Bhogi come to us; may the old depart, may the new enter!"
A traditional Tamil invocation recited during the bonfire and cleaning rituals.

## How others do it

- **Andhra Pradesh / Telangana:** Called Bhogi; the bonfire (Bhogi Mantalu) is a major community event with entire neighbourhoods contributing old items. Bhogi Pallu is especially grand with regi pandlu (jujube berries). Haridasu (travelling bards) visit homes singing.
- **Karnataka:** The eve of Sankranti is observed similarly with bonfires and house cleaning, though less emphasis on the Indra connection.
- **North India:** The equivalent night is Lohri (Punjab) -- bonfire with popcorn, rewri, peanuts, and folk songs. Lohri focuses on fire-worship and the winter solstice rather than harvest purging.

## Who is traditionally exempt

No fasting or strenuous observance is required. Bhogi is about cleaning, discarding, and preparing. Those who are unwell or elderly participate as they can -- even symbolically discarding one old item and lighting a lamp fulfils the spirit of the day.
