# Vinayagar Chaturthi

## In two sentences

The fourth day of the bright fortnight in Bhadrapada, celebrated as the birthday of Vinayagar (Ganesha/Pillaiyar) -- the elephant-headed son of Shiva and Parvati, remover of obstacles, lord of beginnings. A clay murti is installed, worshipped for 1.5, 5, 7, or 10 days, then immersed in water, completing the cycle.

## The story

**Short version.** Shiva and Parvati needed a gatekeeper who could be trusted to guard their home on Mount Kailash. Parvati created a son from the turmeric paste (haridra) she used for her bath, breathed life into him, and set him to guard the entrance while she bathed. Shiva returned and, not knowing the boy, was refused entry. In the altercation, Shiva severed the child's head. Parvati's grief was fierce; Shiva promised restoration. His ganas (attendants) were sent north and returned with the head of the first creature they found -- an elephant. The boy was restored and named Ganesha (Lord of the Ganas).

**Full version.** The Shiva Purana (Rudra Samhita, Kumara Khanda) gives the most complete version. The Ganesh Purana and the Mudgala Purana are dedicated entirely to Ganesha's legends. The Mudgala Purana describes eight avatars of Ganesha, each defeating a different form of human affliction (moha, matsarya, mada -- delusion, jealousy, pride).

Ganesha's two attributes -- the elephant head and the broken tusk -- each carry meaning. The elephant is the animal of wisdom and memory; it does not forget a path and does not turn back once it begins. The broken tusk: in one account, Ganesha used it to write the Mahabharata as Vyasa dictated, when his quill broke. In another, it was broken in a fight with Parashurama. The broken tusk is held as the symbol of sacrifice for knowledge.

The public celebration in its current form was substantially promoted by Bal Gangadhar Tilak in Pune in 1893, who transformed a private household puja into a ten-day community festival as a way to gather people across caste lines during the independence movement. The ten-day format and the grand public Visarjan procession are partly a modern institution built on an ancient base.

Modak -- a sweet steamed dumpling filled with jaggery and coconut -- is Ganesha's favourite food. Its shape, pointed at the top and round at the base, represents cosmic consciousness (Brahman) contained within matter. It is offered at every Ganesha puja.

*Sources: Shiva Purana (Rudra Samhita), Ganesh Purana, Mudgala Purana, Skanda Purana*

## What to do

### Ten-minute version

1. Place an image or a small murti of Ganesha on a clean cloth
2. Offer a red or orange flower (Ganesha's colours), a modak or any sweet, and a few blades of durva grass
3. Light a lamp and incense
4. Chant "Om Gam Ganapataye Namah" 21 times
5. At the end of your chosen days (1.5, 5, or 10), immerse the murti in a bucket of water in the garden, or use an eco-clay murti that dissolves

### Fuller version

**Pranapratishtha (Installation):**
1. Obtain a clay Ganesha murti -- unfired clay or eco-friendly material is strongly preferred for home immersion
2. Choose the duration before you begin: 1.5 days (Derthi/Aadhi), 5 days, 7 days, or 10 days (Anant Chaturdashi)
3. On Chaturthi morning, bathe, wear clean clothes
4. Place the murti on a decorated wooden seat (pat); install with the Pranapratishtha mantra (inviting Ganesha's presence into the murti) -- a priest is traditional, but many families do it themselves
5. Perform Shodashopachara Puja (16 steps of worship): Avahana (welcoming), Asana, Padya (water for feet), Arghya (water for hands), Achamana, Snana (bath), Vastra (cloth), Yajnopavita (sacred thread), Gandha (sandalwood paste), Pushpa (flowers), Dhupa (incense), Deepa (lamp), Naivedya (food offering), Tambula (betel), Pradakshina (circumambulation), Namaskara (prostration)
6. The Modak offering is essential: offer 21 modaks on the day of installation
7. Daily: morning and evening aarti; offer durva (21 blades), red flowers, and a sweet each day

**Daily puja items:**
- Durva grass (21 blades or a bundle) -- this is the most essential item for Ganesha
- Red or orange hibiscus flowers
- Modak (steamed or fried) -- or any sweet
- Red sandalwood paste (rakta chandan) or regular sandalwood
- Akshat (unbroken rice) with turmeric and kumkum

**Aarti:** "Sukhakarta Dukhharta" (Marathi, written by Samarth Ramdas -- the most widely sung Ganesha aarti) or "Jai Ganesh Jai Ganesh Deva" (Hindi, simpler and widely known)

**Visarjan (Immersion):**
1. On the final morning or evening, perform a special farewell puja
2. Carry the murti in procession if possible -- even a walk to the garden with family counts
3. Immerse in a natural body of water (river, ocean, lake) or at home in a large bucket
4. The water from the bucket can be poured at the roots of a tree
5. As the murti dissolves: "Ganpati Bappa Morya, Pudhchya Varshi Lavkar Ya" (Ganesha, come again soon next year)

### Items needed

- Clay or eco-friendly Ganesha murti
- Durva grass -- the single most important item; available at Indian grocers before the festival
- Red/orange flowers (hibiscus is ideal)
- Modak -- fresh or frozen; modak moulds available at Indian stores; rice flour, coconut, and jaggery for homemade
- Red sandalwood paste or sandalwood powder
- Kumkum (red powder), turmeric, akshat (rice)
- Incense, lamp, ghee or coconut oil
- Betel nut and leaves (tambula)
- Coconut

### In Melbourne

Durva grass and clay murtis are the things to source early -- Indian grocers in Footscray, Dandenong, and Box Hill typically stock them in the two weeks before Chaturthi; call ahead. Frozen modak is available from Mithai shops in Dandenong. The Sri Siva Vishnu Temple (Carrum Downs) and the Maharashtrian Mandal of Victoria typically run a multi-day public Ganesh Chaturthi with daily aarti, cultural programmes, and a community Visarjan. Check local Indian community social media groups in July-August for dates and programme details.

For Visarjan: eco-clay murtis that dissolve cleanly in water are widely available and strongly preferred for home use. Port Phillip Bay and the Yarra have been used for community Visarjan in past years, though participants are encouraged to use the smallest possible murti and prefer eco-clay to reduce environmental impact.

## Why

**Scriptural.** Ganesha is Vighneshvara -- the lord of obstacles. He places and removes obstacles. Before any major undertaking -- a journey, a ceremony, a business, a creative work -- Ganesha is invoked first, always. The Rig Veda's Ganapati hymn (RV 2.23.1 -- "Gananam tva ganapati havamahey") is considered his oldest reference.

**Symbolic.** The elephant head: vast memory, patience, the ability to clear a path (elephants are forest engineers). The large ears: he hears all prayers. The small eyes: focused attention. The large belly: he holds the universe. The snake around his waist: mastery of kundalini energy and time. The modak in his hand: the sweetness that awaits at the completion of any challenge.

**The Visarjan.** The immersion of the murti after the festival is not destruction but return -- matter to water, form to formlessness. The tradition teaches that the divine is not contained in the murti permanently; the Pranapratishtha invites presence in, and the Visarjan releases it back. This is a practical and profound teaching about impermanence.

**The broken tusk as the price of knowledge.** Ganesha broke his own tusk to write the Mahabharata when his quill failed. Knowledge has a cost; the willingness to sacrifice something of oneself for it is encoded in his form.

## How others do it

- **Maharashtra:** The largest and most elaborate celebration anywhere; Pune's Kasba Ganpati and Mumbai's Lalbaugcha Raja draw hundreds of thousands; 10-day format is standard; community (sarvajanik) pandals funded by subscription; the Visarjan procession on Anant Chaturdashi is the year's biggest civic event in Mumbai
- **Karnataka (Bengaluru, Mysore):** "Gowri Ganesha" -- Ganesha is preceded by Gowri (Parvati) puja the day before; the two-day festival treats mother and son together; elaborate clay murtis; strong cultural identity with the festival here too
- **Andhra Pradesh / Telangana:** Vinayaka Chavithi; significant public celebrations in Hyderabad; 10-day format common; special Vighnesvara temples packed; the Ganesh procession (Shobha Yatra) closes the festival
- **Tamil Nadu:** Vinayagar Chaturthi; temples perform special abhishekam; modakam (Tamil: kozhukattai) is the prasad; less of a civic street festival than in Maharashtra, but deeply observed at temples
- **North India / diaspora:** More modest household puja; single day or 1.5 days is common; the Mumbai-scale public celebration has spread through TV and diaspora communities

## Who is traditionally exempt

There is a single, unusual exemption specific to Ganesh Chaturthi: the moon (Chandra) is not to be looked at on this night. The Bhavishya Purana recounts that Chandra laughed at Ganesha's rotund form, and Ganesha cursed him -- anyone who sees the moon on Chaturthi night will be falsely accused of theft. If you do see it accidentally, reciting the story of Syamantaka gem (from the Bhagavata Purana, Canto 10) is the traditional remedy. This is the one popular superstition associated with the festival -- it is harmless and rather charming.

For all other exemptions: those who are seriously ill, recently bereaved, or otherwise unable to perform puja simply do what they can. A single flower, a single modak, and the name of Ganesha are sufficient. The Mudgala Purana says Ganesha is pleased more by simplicity and sincerity than by elaborate ritual.
