# Amavasya (New Moon)

## In two sentences

Amavasya is the new moon tithi, the fifteenth of the dark fortnight, when the moon is invisible and the sky is darkest. It is primarily a day for Pitru Tarpan -- offering water and sesame seeds in memory of deceased ancestors -- and for charity; new ventures and celebrations are traditionally deferred to the waxing fortnight.

## Special Amavasyas

Not all new moons carry the same weight. Several are elevated by the month or weekday:

| Name | Condition | Key observance |
|---|---|---|
| Somvati Amavasya | Amavasya falls on Monday | Married women circumambulate a peepal tree; considered highly auspicious for marriage stability |
| Shani Amavasya | Amavasya falls on Saturday | Shani (Saturn) is propitiated; sesame oil lamp lit under peepal tree |
| Mauni Amavasya | Magha Amavasya | Observed with silence (maun vrat) and sacred bathing; the day Sangam bathing at Prayagraj peaks |
| Diwali Amavasya | Ashvina/Kartika Krishna Amavasya | The Lakshmi Puja night of Diwali; see the Diwali page |
| Pitru Amavasya | Ashvina Krishna Amavasya (Sarvapitri) | The final and most important day of Pitru Paksha (Pitra Visarjan) |
| Bhoumi Amavasya | Amavasya in the month of Bhadrapada or as regionally defined | Worship of Bhumi Devi (earth goddess); common in Andhra Pradesh and Odisha |

## The story

**Short version.** The moon is the reservoir of Soma, the nourishing essence that sustains both the living and the dead. On Amavasya, when the moon is dark, the Pitrs (ancestors) are said to draw near, sustained by the offerings of their descendants. The Tarpan (water offering) is a monthly moment of acknowledgement and gratitude.

**Full version.** The Mahabharata (Anushasana Parva) and the Garuda Purana both describe the cosmology of the Pitrs -- ancestors who inhabit Pitru Loka and depend on the ritual nourishment (Shraddha and Tarpan) provided by their descendants. This is not a morbid or fearful belief; the Purana presents it as a relationship of mutual care across the boundary of death.

The Dharmashastra texts (Manusmriti, Yajnavalkya Smriti, and Apastamba Dharmasutra) specify that Tarpan should be performed on Amavasya, on the Pitru Paksha (the dark fortnight of Ashvina), and on the death anniversaries (Tithi Shraddha) of individual ancestors. The monthly Amavasya Tarpan is the baseline -- a brief acknowledgement. The annual Shraddha is the fuller ritual.

The peepal tree (Ficus religiosa) is connected to Amavasya specifically because it is believed that on this day the Pitrs descend and rest in the peepal. Lighting a lamp near a peepal, or circumambulating it, is common practice. The peepal also photosynthesises at night (CAM photosynthesis) -- it genuinely produces oxygen after dark, unlike most trees, which gives the ancient practice an unexpected practical dimension.

*Sources: Mahabharata (Anushasana Parva), Garuda Purana, Manusmriti, Yajnavalkya Smriti, Brahma Purana*

## What to do

### Ten-minute version

1. In the morning or at midday: take a small cup of water, add a pinch of sesame seeds (til) and a few drops of milk
2. Face south (the direction of the Pitrs), and say the names of your deceased parents, grandparents, and any others you remember
3. Pour the water slowly from the cup while saying "I offer this tarpan to [name], may they be at peace" -- or any sincere words in any language
4. Give something in charity today -- food to a person who is hungry, or a small donation
5. Light a lamp in the evening

### Fuller version

**Pitru Tarpan (the main Amavasya ritual):**
1. Bathe before midday
2. Go to a river, lake, or beach if accessible -- flowing or large bodies of water are traditional; a garden or balcony with a vessel of water works at home
3. Fill a copper vessel (or any clean vessel) with water; add sesame seeds and kusha grass if you have them
4. Face south; recite the family gotra (lineage) if known; name the ancestors you are offering to
5. Pour the water three times for each ancestor named while reciting "Tat asmai upatishthatam" or simply their name with the intention of remembrance and gratitude
6. After Tarpan, give food in charity (anna dan) -- this is considered to reach the Pitrs directly

**For those who have lost a parent recently:**
The first Amavasya after a death in the family is particularly significant. Following local customs and consulting a priest or experienced elder is useful -- the rituals vary by region (South Indian, North Indian, Gujarati, Bengali traditions each have their specific forms).

**Somvati Amavasya (Monday new moon) -- additional:**
- Married women circumambulate a peepal tree 108 times, tying a red thread around the trunk
- Offer water to the tree
- This is held to support marital harmony and the wellbeing of the husband

### Items needed

- Clean water vessel (copper preferred)
- Sesame seeds (til) -- a pinch
- Kusha grass (darbha) -- optional but traditional; available at Indian stores
- Food for charity (dana) -- rice, dal, fruit, or a donation

## Why

**Scriptural.** The Garuda Purana is the most explicit text on Pitru obligations. It describes the Pitrs as one of the five great debts (Pancha Maha Rina) that every person owes at birth -- to the gods, to the sages, to the ancestors, to humanity, and to all living beings. Monthly Tarpan is the baseline repayment of the ancestral debt.

**Symbolic.** The dark moon is the moment when the cycle rests before renewal. The tradition uses this void not as loss but as space -- a space to remember those who came before, without whom the current life would not exist. It is an anti-consumerist day: not a day for acquiring or beginning, but for acknowledging.

**Why no new beginnings.** The Muhurta texts (Muhurta Chintamani and others) classify Amavasya as a parva day unsuitable for beginnings -- weddings, groundbreakings, and major purchases are deferred. This is not superstition but a built-in pause: one day a month for looking back rather than forward.

**Physiological lore.** Ayurvedic texts associate the new moon with a dip in Soma (the nourishing lunar essence). Traditional advice includes eating lightly, sleeping adequately, and avoiding intoxicants. This is a traditional view; make your own judgement.

## How others do it

- **South India (Tamil tradition):** Amavasai is observed with significant temple visits; Mahalaya Amavasai (in the Tamil month of Purattasi, equivalent to Pitru Paksha Amavasya) is the most important; bathing in the sea or a river at dawn, followed by Tarpan and feeding Brahmins or the poor
- **Bengal:** Amavasya is associated with Kali worship in Shakta households; lamps and offerings to the fierce Goddess are part of the evening
- **Maharashtra:** Pitru Tarpan at the river is common; women observe Somvati Amavasya specifically with peepal tree worship
- **Gujarat:** Families gather for communal Tarpan; charity (food distribution) is prominent
- **North India:** Bathing at river ghats; lamps floated on the river at dusk

## Who is traditionally exempt

There are no mandatory fasts for Amavasya in most traditions -- fasting is optional. Those who are unwell, pregnant, nursing, or elderly are exempt from any fast. The Tarpan can be simplified to its most basic form -- a cup of water poured with intention -- and this is considered fully valid regardless of circumstances. Children are not expected to perform Tarpan; it becomes meaningful when one is an adult with one's own experience of loss.
