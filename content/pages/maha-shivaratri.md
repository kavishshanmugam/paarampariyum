# Maha Shivaratri

## In two sentences

The fourteenth night of the waning moon in Magha (amanta) / Phalguna (purnimanta), when devotees keep a night-long vigil, fast, and offer Abhishek to Shiva with milk, water, and bael leaves. It is considered the night when Shiva performs his cosmic dance and when the formless divine is most accessible.

## In Melbourne

The engine computes this for Melbourne's coordinates and AEDT timezone. In India this falls in late winter, typically February or early March; in Melbourne it arrives in high summer. The vigil from dusk to dawn spans roughly 9 p.m. to 6 a.m. AEDT -- a long but very manageable night.

## The story

**Short version.** Shiva is the transformer, the yogi, the lord of all that is auspicious. This night he is said to have appeared as an infinite pillar of light (the Jyotirlinga), humbling both Brahma and Vishnu. It is also understood as the night before the world's creation, when Shiva dances the Tandava.

**Full version.** The Shiva Purana gives several accounts. In one, Brahma and Vishnu argued over who was supreme; Shiva manifested as a blazing column of light with no top or bottom -- the first Jyotirlinga -- and both gods were humbled. This is why twelve Jyotirlingas are especially sacred. In another account, a hunter stranded in a forest accidentally kept vigil and offered bael leaves to a Shivalinga all night without knowing it; his unintentional devotion was rewarded. This story (found in the Skanda Purana) is often cited to show that sincerity matters more than ritual perfection. Shivaratri literally means "the night of Shiva" -- the dark night of the waning moon when there is minimal lunar influence and the mind can be stilled most easily.

The Linga Purana and Shiva Purana both specify that the Chaturdashi tithi of Krishna Paksha in Magha/Phalguna is the Maha Shivaratri, distinct from the monthly Shivaratri that falls on every Krishna Chaturdashi.

*Sources: Shiva Purana, Linga Purana, Skanda Purana*

## What to do

### Ten-minute version

1. Fast during the day if able, or eat only fruit
2. At night, bathe a Shivalinga (or a photo/murti) with water, then milk
3. Offer one or three bael (bilva) leaves -- fresh if possible, dry is acceptable
4. Light incense and a lamp
5. Chant "Om Namah Shivaya" for a few minutes
6. Stay awake past midnight if you can, even briefly

### Fuller version

The night is traditionally divided into four prahars (watches), each about three hours. A short puja is performed at each watch.

1. Prepare by fasting from the morning -- water is permitted; many take fruit and milk
2. Bathe before the first prahar begins (around dusk)
3. Set up a clean altar with a Shivalinga or image of Shiva
4. Perform Panchamrit Abhishek: bathe the linga successively with milk, yoghurt, ghee, honey, and sugar, then plain water
5. After each Abhishek, offer bael leaves in sets of three, flowers (white flowers are traditional -- dhatura if available, but any white flower works), and fruit
6. Recite the Shiva Panchakshara Stotra ("Om Namah Shivaya") or the Rudri (Shri Rudram from the Yajurveda) if you know it
7. Read or listen to stories from the Shiva Purana through the night
8. Stay awake for all four prahars if possible; if not, the midnight prahar is the most important
9. Break the fast the next morning after bathing and a final prayer

### Items needed

- Shivalinga (stone, brass, or clay) or image of Shiva
- Milk, yoghurt, ghee, honey, sugar (for Panchamrit)
- Bael (bilva) leaves -- fresh or dried
- White flowers (jasmine, white rose, dhatura if available)
- Fruits (banana, apple)
- Incense and an oil lamp
- Water in a small copper or steel vessel

### In Melbourne

Bael leaves are the trickiest item. Indian grocery stores in Footscray, Dandenong, and Box Hill occasionally carry dried bilva leaves; check a few days before. The BAPS Swaminarayan Temple (Glen Waverley) and the Shiva temples in Carrum Downs and Sunshine hold Abhishek programmes on this night -- attending one is the easiest way to observe fully. Many run from 9 p.m. to 5 a.m. with communal vigil and multiple Abhishek sessions.

## Why

**Scriptural.** Shiva's manifestation as the Jyotirlinga; the Chaturdashi of Krishna Paksha as the natural time when Shiva's energy is most potent (Shiva Purana, Vidyeshvara Samhita).

**Symbolic.** The dark waning moon night is understood as the dissolution phase of the cosmic cycle -- Shiva's domain. Staying awake through the night mirrors Shiva's own sleepless state as Mahadeva. Bael leaves, associated with Shiva, are cooling and offered in groups of three representing the trinity (srishti, sthiti, laya -- creation, preservation, dissolution).

**Seasonal.** In India, late winter/early spring is the junction of seasons (sandhi), considered a powerful time for tapas (austerity) and inner work. The fast and vigil are a form of accessible tapas for householders.

## How others do it

- **Shaivite traditions (South India):** Elaborate temple Abhisheks through the night; Shri Rudram chanted continuously; Shiva devotees in North Tamil Nadu carry kavadi
- **Kashmiri Shaivism:** Herath (the local name) is the biggest festival of the year; families fast and perform puja with walnut-based sweets
- **Shaiva Siddhanta:** The four prahars are observed rigorously with specific mantras for each
- **Lingayat tradition (Karnataka):** Deep personal identification with Shiva; simple Lingayat puja at the ishtalinga
- **Unmarried women:** Many young women fast and pray specifically for a husband who is like Shiva -- devoted, steady, ascetic in nature. Married women pray for the health and long life of their husbands.
- **Nepal:** Pashupatinath temple in Kathmandu draws hundreds of thousands; sadhus gather from across South Asia

## Who is traditionally exempt

Those who are seriously ill, pregnant women (full fast is not advised), small children, and the elderly are exempt from the full fast. Partial observance -- avoiding grains, staying awake for the midnight hour only -- is considered fully valid. The Shiva Purana specifically says that Shiva values intention over form.
