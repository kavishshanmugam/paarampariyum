# Dev Uthani Ekadashi

## In two sentences

The Ekadashi on which Vishnu is said to awaken from his four-month cosmic sleep (Chaturmas). Weddings and other auspicious ceremonies resume after this day.

## In Melbourne

The engine computes this for Melbourne's coordinates and AEDT timezone. This falls in November, about two weeks after Diwali. In Melbourne it marks late spring. The auspicious wedding season that follows extends through summer -- the opposite of the Indian calendar where weddings cluster before the monsoon.

## The story

**Short version.** Vishnu sleeps on the cosmic serpent Shesha from Devshayani Ekadashi (Ashadha) to this day. His awakening signals that the gods are once again fully attentive, and auspicious activities can resume.

**Full version.** According to the Padma Purana and Vishnu Purana, Vishnu enters Yoga Nidra (cosmic sleep) on the Ekadashi of Shukla Paksha in Ashadha (June-July), which coincides with the start of the monsoon and Chaturmas. During this period, weddings, house-warmings, and other shubh (auspicious) ceremonies are traditionally avoided. On Dev Uthani Ekadashi, Vishnu awakens. The Tulsi Vivah ceremony -- the symbolic marriage of the Tulsi plant to Vishnu (as Shaligram) -- is performed on this day or the next, marking the formal opening of the wedding season.

*Sources: Padma Purana, Vishnu Purana, Bhagavata Purana*

## What to do

### Ten-minute version

1. Observe Ekadashi fast (avoid grains; fruit and milk are fine)
2. Light a lamp near a Tulsi plant
3. Offer flowers and water to Tulsi
4. Say "Om Namo Narayanaya" and express gratitude for the season's turning

### Fuller version

1. Fast from the previous evening (avoid grains, beans, onion, garlic)
2. Wake before sunrise. Bathe and wear clean clothes
3. Draw a small rangoli near the Tulsi plant
4. Decorate the Tulsi with flowers, turmeric, and kumkum
5. If performing Tulsi Vivah: place a Shaligram stone beside the Tulsi, tie a thread around both, offer sweets, and circle the plant
6. Recite the Vishnu Sahasranama or at minimum the mantra "Om Namo Bhagavate Vasudevaya"
7. Light sugarcane stalks around the Tulsi (symbolic of celebration)
8. Break fast the next morning (Dwadashi) within the parana window (after sunrise, before a quarter of the day has passed)

### Items needed

- Tulsi plant (holy basil, commonly available in Melbourne nurseries)
- Oil lamp or diya
- Flowers (marigold or any available)
- Turmeric and kumkum
- Shaligram stone (optional, for Tulsi Vivah)
- Sugarcane sticks (optional)

### In Melbourne

Tulsi plants grow well in Melbourne's climate from September to April. Indian grocers carry Shaligram stones. If you don't have a Tulsi plant, a small basil plant is a reasonable stand-in.

## Why

**Scriptural.** Vishnu's awakening marks the end of Chaturmas. The Padma Purana prescribes this Ekadashi as especially meritorious.

**Symbolic.** The cycle of divine sleep and awakening mirrors nature's rhythm of rest and renewal. Activities paused during the monsoon resume.

**Social-historical.** The "wedding season" in Hindu tradition begins after this day. Families plan weddings, engagements, and house-warmings from this Ekadashi onward.

## How others do it

- **North India:** Tulsi Vivah is widely observed. Sugarcane and lights are arranged around the Tulsi
- **Maharashtra:** Tulsi Vivah is a significant community event, sometimes with full wedding celebrations for the plant
- **Vaishnavas:** This Ekadashi is one of the most important in the Vaishnava calendar

## Who is traditionally exempt

Standard Ekadashi exemptions apply: children under 8, the elderly, pregnant women, nursing mothers, and the unwell are exempt from fasting. Remembrance and a simple offering are always enough.
