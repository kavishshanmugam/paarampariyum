# Aadi Perukku

## In two sentences

A Tamil celebration of water and fertility on the 18th of the month Aadi, when rivers are in flood and the earth is being nourished by the monsoon. Women are the primary participants -- they prepare offerings, go to a river or body of water, and set food afloat on banana leaves as thanks.

## In Melbourne

Aadi Perukku falls around August 2-3, mid-winter in Melbourne with good rainfall -- the water-abundance metaphor is not entirely mismatched. The Yarra River, Maribyrnong River, or Port Phillip Bay can serve as the water body. Community groups sometimes organise Aadi Perukku picnics at parks near water.

## The story

**Short version.** "Perukku" means "rising" or "swelling" -- the rivers are in flood. The festival thanks the river goddesses and Amman (the Mother Goddess) for nourishing the land. It is one of the oldest agrarian water festivals in Tamil tradition.

**Full version.** Aadi Perukku has no single Puranic narrative. It is rooted in the Tamil agricultural cycle. The Aadi month is the heart of the southwest monsoon; the Kaveri, Vaigai, and Thamiraparani rivers swell with rainwater. Farmers begin sowing paddy.

The Silappathikaram (one of the five great Tamil epics, circa 2nd century CE) describes festivals along riverbanks that correspond to Aadi Perukku celebrations. The Aadi month is traditionally considered inauspicious for new ventures (no weddings, no housewarming) but highly auspicious for agricultural activities and water-related rituals.

Women go to the riverbank, prepare offerings of sweet pongal and other foods, set them afloat on banana leaves, and pray for the prosperity of the family. The water-play by women on this day is one of the rare festival occasions where women's water-play is ritually sanctioned.

*Sources: Silappathikaram, Tamil Sangam literature, regional temple traditions*

## What to do

### Ten-minute version

1. Cook sweet pongal (rice + jaggery + milk)
2. Place a portion on a banana leaf with flowers, turmeric, and kumkum
3. Go to a body of water (river, lake, beach, or even a bowl of water at home) and set the leaf afloat or place the offering at the water's edge
4. Pray for the well-being of the family

### Fuller version

1. Cook a variety of rice preparations: sweet pongal, ellu sadam (sesame rice), coconut rice, lemon rice, tamarind rice -- five different rice preparations is traditional
2. Women wear new clothes (traditionally green or yellow -- colours of fertility and the monsoon)
3. Go to the river, lake, or beach with family
4. Arrange the food on banana leaves as offerings
5. Float the offerings on the water with flowers, turmeric, kumkum, and bangles
6. Women play in the water -- this is ritually significant
7. Sing folk songs about the river and the monsoon
8. Families picnic at the waterside
9. In temple towns, special pujas are performed for Amman and for the river

### Items needed

- Rice, jaggery, milk, ghee for sweet pongal
- Sesame seeds, coconut, lemon, tamarind for variety rice dishes
- Banana leaves
- Flowers, turmeric, kumkum
- Glass bangles (traditionally set afloat with the offering)
- New clothes (optional, green or yellow preferred)

### In Melbourne

August is mid-winter with cold water, so the water-play aspect may be limited, but the food-offering and prayer aspects are fully doable. Banana leaves are available year-round at Vietnamese/Sri Lankan grocers in Footscray and Springvale. All rice and spice ingredients are standard stock at Indian grocers.

## Why

**Scriptural.** The Rig Veda's Nadi Sukta (RV 10.75) praises rivers as life-givers. Tamil Sangam poetry extensively honours rivers. Aadi Perukku is the living embodiment of this river-reverence tradition.

**Symbolic.** Water = life, fertility, purification. The offerings set afloat = gratitude returned to the source. Bangles = a woman's married status and well-being; setting them afloat = entrusting the family's welfare to the divine feminine in the water.

**Seasonal.** In Tamil Nadu, Aadi is peak monsoon. In Melbourne, August has good rainfall -- the water-abundance metaphor maps reasonably well.

## How others do it

- This is a specifically Tamil festival, most prominent along the Kaveri river basin (Thanjavur, Trichy, Kumbakonam districts)
- Urban observance is simpler: temple visit, food preparation, small water offering

## Who is traditionally exempt

No fasting. No restrictions. This is a celebration of abundance. Anyone can participate.
