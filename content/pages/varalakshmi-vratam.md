# Varalakshmi Vratam

## In two sentences

A vow to Lakshmi performed on the Friday before Shravana Purnima, primarily by married women, seeking the Goddess's blessings for the family's health, prosperity, and well-being. The sacred thread (saradu) with nine knots -- each representing a form of Lakshmi -- is tied on the right wrist after the puja.

## In Melbourne

Varalakshmi Vratam falls in July-August, mid-winter in Melbourne. Telugu, Tamil, and Kannada community groups organise collective pujas. Most items are standard at Indian grocers. The sacred thread (saradu) can be made at home with cotton thread dyed with turmeric, tied with nine knots.

## The story

**Short version.** The goddess Lakshmi appeared to a devout woman named Charumati in a dream and instructed her in this vrata. Charumati told her friends; they all observed it. Lakshmi blessed them with prosperity, good children, and harmony.

**Full version.** The Skanda Purana (Varalakshmi Vrata Katha) narrates: in the city of Kundina, Charumati was visited by Lakshmi who told her to observe the Varalakshmi Vratam on the Friday before Shravana Purnima. The "Vara" means "boon-granting" -- this is Lakshmi as the giver of boons.

The vrata invokes the Ashtalakshmi (eight forms of Lakshmi): Adi Lakshmi (origin), Dhana Lakshmi (wealth), Dhanya Lakshmi (grain), Gaja Lakshmi (royalty), Santana Lakshmi (children), Veera Lakshmi (courage), Vijaya Lakshmi (victory), Vidya Lakshmi (knowledge). These represent comprehensive well-being, not just monetary wealth.

*Sources: Skanda Purana (Varalakshmi Vrata section), Lakshmi Tantra*

## What to do

### Ten-minute version

1. Set up an image or kalasham (brass pot with coconut on top) representing Lakshmi
2. Tie a new yellow or red thread (saradu) with 9 knots on the right wrist
3. Offer flowers, fruits, turmeric, and kumkum to Lakshmi
4. Light a lamp and pray for family well-being

### Fuller version

1. **Morning:** Oil bath; wear a new sari or best clothes (yellow or red)
2. **Setup:** Place a kalasham -- brass pot filled with water and coins, topped with mango leaves and coconut, draped with yellow/red cloth
3. Draw a Lakshmi pada (footprint) rangoli at the entrance
4. **Perform the puja:** Invoke Lakshmi into the kalasham; perform shodashopachara (16-step puja)
5. Offer the nine items: turmeric, kumkum, bangles, flowers, fruits, betel leaves, coconut, new cloth, and the saradu
6. **The saradu:** Yellow thread with 9 knots, each representing a form of Lakshmi. Tied on the right wrist after the puja.
7. **Naivedyam:** Panakam (jaggery drink), sundal, sweet pongal
8. **Read the Varalakshmi Vrata Katha** (the story of Charumati)
9. **Give tambulam** to married women guests (betel leaf + areca nut + turmeric + kumkum + fruits + bangles)

### Items needed

- Brass kalasham, mango leaves, coconut
- Yellow/red thread for saradu (9 knots)
- Turmeric, kumkum
- Glass bangles (green or red)
- Betel leaves, areca nut
- Flowers (lotus preferred, any will do)
- Fruits, jaggery
- New sari or blouse piece

### In Melbourne

Most items are standard at Indian grocers in Dandenong, Footscray, and Glen Waverley. Some Indian stores sell pre-made Varalakshmi saradu before Shravana. Telugu and Kannada community groups organise collective pujas.

## Why

**Scriptural.** The Skanda Purana prescribes this vrata. The Ashtalakshmi represent complete well-being -- not just wealth but health, progeny, courage, victory, and knowledge.

**Symbolic.** The nine knots = nine forms of Lakshmi. The thread on the wrist = Lakshmi's protection. The kalasham = the ocean from which Lakshmi emerged.

## How others do it

- **Tamil Nadu:** Strong observance; saradu thread is central; elaborate kolam
- **Andhra Pradesh / Telangana:** Possibly the most popular women's festival; elaborate setups
- **Karnataka:** Called Varamahalakshmi; equally popular; mango leaf torana emphasised
- **Kerala:** Not widely observed
- **North India:** No equivalent (the closest is Lakshmi Puja on Diwali night, very different in character)

## Who is traditionally exempt

Traditionally performed by married women. Unmarried women may perform it for future prosperity. The restriction on widows is widely relaxed in modern practice. Men participate but do not typically lead the vrata. No mandatory fast, though some women eat only one meal.
