# Makar Sankranti

## In two sentences

The moment the sun crosses into sidereal Capricorn (Makara rashi), computed using the Lahiri ayanamsa -- typically around 14 January each year -- marking the beginning of Uttarayana, the sun's northward journey. It is the only major Hindu festival fixed to a solar event rather than the lunar calendar, and is celebrated as a harvest festival under many names across the subcontinent.

## The story

**Short version.** On Uttarayana, the sun begins its northward arc. The Mahabharata says that Bhishma Pitamah, mortally wounded on the battlefield, lay on a bed of arrows for weeks waiting for Uttarayana to begin before releasing his life, knowing that dying in the sun's northward phase is auspicious. This is why Makar Sankranti carries weight beyond harvest -- it is also a day for the ancestors.

**Full version.** The Mahabharata (Anushasana Parva, also the framing of Bhishma Parva) describes Bhishma, who had the boon of choosing his time of death, waiting on his arrow-bed from near the end of the war until Uttarayana. The Srimad Bhagavata Purana (Canto 1) also mentions Uttarayana as the time when yogis prefer to leave the body, as the sun's northward path is associated with the path of light (devayana) rather than the path of return (pitriyana).

Makar Sankranti has deep astronomical grounding: the sun's ingress into Capricorn in the sidereal zodiac (as opposed to the tropical zodiac used in Western astrology) is computed using the Lahiri ayanamsa, the standard for the Hindu calendar. The day is also Surya Sankranti -- a day to honour the sun directly with arghya (water offering).

The regional harvest dimension is equally ancient. The Vedic calendar was agricultural; the Sankranti in the depth of winter (India) or summer (Australia) coincides with the period when crops are gathered and plenty is most visible.

*Sources: Mahabharata (Bhishma Parva, Anushasana Parva), Srimad Bhagavata Purana (Canto 1), Surya Siddhanta*

## What to do

### Ten-minute version

1. At sunrise, step outside (or face a window) and offer a small cup of water to the sun -- raise it toward the sun and let it fall, while saying "Om Suryaya Namah"
2. Eat til-gul (sesame and jaggery sweets) -- if you don't have them, a spoon of sesame seeds with a pinch of jaggery works
3. Say to someone: "Til gul ghya, god god bola" (Marathi: take til-gul and speak sweetly) -- the idiom of reconciliation on this day

### Fuller version

1. Wake before sunrise; bathe before the sun rises
2. Perform the sun offering (Surya Arghya): fill a copper vessel with water and pour it from a height toward the rising sun while chanting the Surya mantra or the Gayatri Mantra
3. Prepare or obtain the regional sweet: til-gul laddoo (Maharashtra), til chikki (North India), khichdi (UP, Bihar), Pongal (Tamil Nadu)
4. Donate sesame seeds, jaggery, blankets, or warm clothing -- charity on Sankranti day is considered especially meritorious in the Dharmashastra texts
5. Fly a kite if you celebrate the Gujarati or Rajasthani way -- even briefly, with children
6. In the evening, light a lamp and offer thanks to the sun for the return of its northward journey

### Items needed

- Copper vessel for Surya Arghya (a steel glass works)
- Sesame seeds (til) and jaggery (gur) -- for sweets or as raw offering
- Regional sweet: til laddoo, chikki, Pongal ingredients (rice, moong dal, jaggery, ghee), or khichdi
- Kite and string (optional, for Uttarayan / Makar Sankranti kite tradition)

## Why

**Scriptural.** Uttarayana is the sun's northward journey, held in the Bhagavata Purana to be spiritually superior to Dakshinayana (southward). Bhishma's choice to die on this day is the canonical example of its significance.

**Astronomical.** Makar Sankranti is anchored to the sidereal zodiac, not the seasons. It is one of the few Hindu observances where the date is computed from the actual position of the sun against fixed stars (with the Lahiri ayanamsa correction), not from the moon. This makes it unusually precise and rarely debated.

**Symbolic.** The sun entering Capricorn marks the beginning of longer days in the Northern Hemisphere. The sesame-jaggery sweets combine warmth (jaggery) and nutrition (sesame) -- practical foods for winter in India. Offering them is also an act of social sweetness: tradition says harsh words spoken on this day are "taken back" with til-gul.

**Harvest.** Pongal (Tamil Nadu), Lohri (Punjab, the night before), Bihu (Assam), Bhogi (Andhra/Telangana), Uttarayan (Gujarat) -- these are all harvest festivals falling within days of each other, reflecting the subcontinent-wide importance of this solar moment to agricultural communities.

## How others do it

- **Tamil Nadu -- Pongal:** A four-day festival. Bhogi on the eve (burning of old things); Pongal day with fresh rice boiled in new pots until it overflows (the overflow is auspicious -- "Pongal-o Pongal!"); Mattu Pongal (thanksgiving to cattle); Kaanum Pongal (family visits). The sweet Pongal (chakkarai pongal) with jaggery and ghee is the essential dish.
- **Maharashtra -- Til-Gul:** Neighbours exchange til-gul laddoo and chikki; the phrase "til gul ghya, god god bola" (take sesame-jaggery, speak sweetly) is the day's social greeting; kite flying in some regions
- **Gujarat -- Uttarayan:** Perhaps the most festive expression; the entire state flies kites for two days (14-15 January); rooftop kite battles; the International Kite Festival in Ahmedabad; evening patang (kite) lit with small LED lights
- **Punjab -- Lohri:** Celebrated the night before; a bonfire where sesame seeds, popcorn, and rewri are thrown into the fire; folk songs (Sundar Mundriye); associated with the birth of sons and newlyweds
- **Assam -- Bihu:** Magh Bihu (Bhogali Bihu) -- community feast, meji bonfire, traditional games, and the preparation of pitha (rice cakes) and laru (sesame balls)
- **Andhra Pradesh / Telangana -- Bhogi:** Old and worn items are burned in a bonfire on the eve; Pongal-style rice dish prepared

## Who is traditionally exempt

There are no mandatory fasts associated with Makar Sankranti -- it is primarily a celebratory and charitable day. Charity (dan) on this day is the most widely mentioned obligation across traditions: donating sesame, jaggery, blankets, or warm clothing to those in need. All ages and health conditions participate in whatever way they can.
