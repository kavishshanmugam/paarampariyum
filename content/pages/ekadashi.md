# Ekadashi

## In two sentences

The eleventh tithi of each fortnight — occurring twice a month, in both the waxing and waning lunar cycles — is sacred to Vishnu and observed with a fast from grains. It is one of the most widely kept recurring observances in Vaishnavism, and is also observed by many Smarta Hindus.

## In Melbourne

Because tithi boundaries shift against the clock, Melbourne's Ekadashi may differ by a day from the India date — always use the app's computed date rather than calendars printed in India. The fast is broken on Dwadashi (the twelfth tithi), after sunrise the following morning, making this effectively a 24-hour observance across two calendar days.

## The story

**Short version.** According to the Padma Purana, Vishnu was engaged in battle with a demon named Mura. Exhausted, he rested in a cave. A divine energy emerged from his body and slew Mura. Vishnu named this energy Ekadashi and granted her the boon that anyone who fasts on the eleventh tithi will be dear to him.

**Full version.** The Padma Purana contains the foundational story: the demon Mura (or Murasura) had conquered the three worlds and even the gods could not defeat him. Vishnu fought him for thousands of years and finally retreated to rest in Hemavati cave. There, a supremely beautiful woman emerged from Vishnu's own form — a divine Shakti, the collective energy of his weapons. She challenged and killed Mura while Vishnu slept. When Vishnu woke and asked her name, she had none. He named her Ekadashi (the eleventh) because she was born on the eleventh tithi, and told her to ask for a boon. She asked that those who observe this day in fasting and worship of Vishnu be granted moksha. Vishnu agreed — and thus Ekadashi became one of the most liberation-conferring observances in Vaishnavism. Each of the 24 Ekadashis across the Hindu lunar year has its own name, legend, and specific merits described in the Puranas (e.g., Nirjala Ekadashi, Vaikunta Ekadashi, Devshayani Ekadashi).

*Sources: Padma Purana (Uttara Khanda), Vishnu Purana, Skanda Purana*

## What to do

### Ten-minute version

1. Skip grains for the day (rice, wheat, dal) — fruits, milk, and nuts are freely eaten
2. Spend a few minutes in prayer or recitation of Vishnu's names
3. The following morning (Dwadashi), eat a proper meal after sunrise to break the fast properly

### Fuller version

1. The evening before Ekadashi (Dashami), eat a light dinner and avoid non-vegetarian food
2. On Ekadashi, wake early and bathe
3. Fast from all grains, lentils, and legumes for the day
   - Permitted: fruits, milk, yoghurt, ghee, sugar, nuts, sendha namak (rock salt), potatoes, sweet potatoes, sabudana (tapioca)
   - Avoided: rice, wheat, barley, lentils, beans, regular salt (in strict observance), onion, garlic
4. Offer Vishnu (or your family deity) tulsi leaves, flowers, and a lamp
5. Recite the Vishnu Sahasranama, the Bhagavad Gita (Chapter 2 or any chapter), or simply repeat "Om Namo Narayanaya" or "Hare Krishna" throughout the day
6. Read the Ekadashi Mahatmya (story of that specific Ekadashi) if available
7. Stay awake for some of the night in prayer or kirtan if able (Jagaran) — especially on Vaikunta Ekadashi
8. On Dwadashi morning, break the fast after sunrise with a light meal; do not break it after sunset or before sunrise as this is considered to negate the merit

### Items needed

- Fruit and permissible foods for the day
- Tulsi leaves (sacred to Vishnu)
- A copy of the Vishnu Sahasranama or Bhagavad Gita (optional)
- Oil lamp and incense for evening puja

## Why

**Scriptural.** The Padma Purana states: "One who fasts on Ekadashi, even unknowingly, attains the merit of performing Ashvamedha yaga." The day is considered so potent that even the wicked who fast accidentally are said to benefit. Vaikunta Ekadashi (Margashira Shukla Ekadashi) is held to be the most important of all.

**Symbolic.** Fasting from grains lightens the body; the reduced digestive load is understood in Ayurveda to clarify the mind. The fast is not deprivation — it is redirection of energy from digestion toward contemplation.

**Physiological.** Twice-monthly fasting from grains is consistent with periodic caloric restriction, which has a growing body of evidence for metabolic benefit. The ancient prescription aligns in broad strokes with modern intermittent fasting research, though the tradition's purpose is devotional rather than physiological.

*Evidence grade: Plausible alignment; no direct study of Ekadashi-specific outcomes.*

## How others do it

- **Strict Vaishnavas (ISKCON, Gaudiya tradition):** Nirjala fast (no water) on at least one Ekadashi per year (Nirjala Ekadashi in summer); all other Ekadashis observed with full grain abstention
- **Smartas:** Many observe Ekadashi but with less stringency; some skip only rice; some observe only certain named Ekadashis
- **Shaiva households:** Ekadashi is less central; Pradosh Vrat on Trayodashi is the fortnightly parallel for Shiva devotees
- **South India:** Vaikunta Ekadashi (in Margashira) is a major temple event; queues at Venkateswara temples stretch through the night

## Who is traditionally exempt

Children under 8, those who are ill, pregnant or nursing women, and the elderly who cannot safely fast are traditionally exempt. They may eat normally or take only fruit and milk. A symbolic partial fast (giving up one meal, or fasting from rice only) is always acceptable and considered to carry partial merit. The tradition explicitly states that intent matters: a sincere partial fast is better than a grudging full one.
