# Holi

## In two sentences

The full moon of Phalguna marks Holika Dahan -- a bonfire lit at dusk to enact the destruction of the demoness Holika and the triumph of devotion over cruelty. The next morning is Rangwali Holi, when people drench each other in coloured powder and water, celebrating spring and the playful love of Radha and Krishna.

## In Melbourne

The engine computes this for Melbourne's coordinates and AEDT timezone. In India Holi heralds the end of winter; in Melbourne it falls in mid-autumn (typically March), so the weather is cooling rather than warming. The colour play is just as festive regardless -- Melbourne's Indian community runs Holi events at parks and community halls, often on the Saturday closest to the date.

## The story

**Short version.** The demon king Hiranyakashipu commanded all to worship him and not Vishnu. His own son Prahlada refused, devotion absolute. Hiranyakashipu ordered his sister Holika -- who had a boon making her immune to fire -- to sit in flames with Prahlada on her lap. The boon turned: Holika burned; Prahlada walked out unharmed. The bonfire remembers her end.

**Full version.** The Prahlada story is told in the Bhagavata Purana (Canto 7) and the Vishnu Purana. Hiranyakashipu, having received a boon of near-immortality, grew arrogant and forbade the worship of Vishnu throughout his kingdom. Prahlada, a child, remained steadfast. After multiple attempts on his life failed -- thrown from a cliff, trampled by elephants, bitten by serpents -- Hiranyakashipu devised the Holika plan. Holika's boon, however, only held when she entered fire alone; using it to harm an innocent cancelled it. Prahlada's chanting of Vishnu's name through the ordeal is held up as the archetype of unflinching bhakti.

The second layer of Holi is Vaishnava -- the colour play between Radha and Krishna in Vrindavan, described in the Gita Govinda and later Bhakti poetry. Krishna, dark-skinned, playfully smeared colour on the fair Radha; this became the template for the joyful abandon of Rangwali Holi. The Braj region (Mathura-Vrindavan) is famous for Lathmar Holi, where the women of Barsana chase the men of Nandgaon with sticks, a boisterous re-enactment of that divine play.

*Sources: Bhagavata Purana (Canto 7), Vishnu Purana, Narada Purana, Gita Govinda*

## What to do

### Ten-minute version

1. In the evening of Purnima: light a small candle or lamp to mark Holika Dahan and recall Prahlada's story
2. Next morning: wear old white clothes and play with colour with family at home
3. Prepare or buy gujiya (sweet dumplings) and thandai (if desired)
4. Greet people with "Holi Hai!" and share sweets

### Fuller version

**Holika Dahan (Purnima evening):**
1. If there is a community bonfire nearby, attend it -- the collective energy is the point
2. If at home, light a small fire or a cluster of lamps in a safe outdoor spot
3. Perform a simple pradakshina (circumambulation) around the fire while praying
4. Throw a handful of dried coconut or sesame seeds into the flames as an offering
5. The fire represents the burning of ego, cruelty, and attachment

**Rangwali Holi (next morning):**
1. Play starts after sunrise and traditionally ends by midday
2. Use natural plant-based colours (gulal) rather than synthetic ones -- they are gentler on skin and eyes
3. Apply a thin layer of coconut oil to skin and hair before playing -- it makes cleanup easier
4. Prepare or purchase gujiya, puran poli, mathri, or other traditional sweets to share
5. Thandai (cold spiced milk with cardamom, fennel, and rose) is the traditional drink; bhang thandai is a traditional intoxicant in some regional contexts -- use discretion

### Items needed

- Gulal (coloured powder) -- natural/herbal preferred
- Water guns (pichkari) or water balloons for water play
- Old white clothes (white shows colour best)
- Coconut oil (pre-apply)
- Gujiya or sweets to share
- Small candle or lamp for Holika Dahan if no community bonfire

### In Melbourne

Indian grocery stores in Footscray, Dandenong, and Box Hill stock gulal and gujiya mix in the weeks before Holi. The Federation of Indian Associations of Victoria (FIAV) and various temple committees typically organise a community Holi event at a park -- check local Indian community social media groups in February for dates. The Sri Siva Vishnu Temple in Carrum Downs and the BAPS temple in Glen Waverley both observe the festival.

## Why

**Scriptural.** The defeat of Holika enacts the Bhagavata Purana's central teaching: devotion (bhakti) is a shield that no worldly power can penetrate. The bonfire is a ritual memory, not superstition.

**Symbolic.** Colour erases social distinctions -- for one morning, hierarchy collapses. The Bhakti movement used Holi imagery extensively to express the dissolution of the self in divine love.

**Seasonal.** In India, Phalguna Purnima marks the arrival of spring (Vasant). Fields are beginning to bloom; the harvest of Rabi crops (wheat, mustard) is weeks away. Colour play mirrors the natural explosion of colour in the landscape.

## How others do it

- **Braj (Mathura-Vrindavan):** Lathmar Holi begins a week early; Phoolon wali Holi (flowers instead of colour) at Vrindavan's Banke Bihari temple; the celebration lasts a full week
- **Bengal:** Dol Yatra / Dol Purnima -- images of Radha-Krishna are swung in decorated swings and taken in procession; associated with the birth of Chaitanya Mahaprabhu
- **Rajasthan:** Royal Holi with elephant processions in Jaipur; Shekawati region has elaborate fresco-adorned haveli settings
- **Punjab:** Hola Mohalla, started by Guru Gobind Singh, is celebrated by Sikhs a day later with martial arts, poetry, and music -- a different spirit to the colour play
- **South India:** Less widely observed as a major festival; Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh have their own spring festivals (Ugadi follows shortly after)

## Who is traditionally exempt

Infants, the frail, and those with respiratory conditions or skin sensitivities should stay indoors or observe only Holika Dahan. Natural colours (plant-based gulal) are far safer than synthetic ones for everyone. Playing gently -- tika on the forehead rather than full drenching -- is always an acceptable form of participation.
