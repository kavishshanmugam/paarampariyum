# Pradosh Vrat

## In two sentences

Observed on the Trayodashi (thirteenth tithi) of both the waxing and waning fortnight each month, Pradosh Vrat is dedicated to Shiva and Parvati and its central act is worship during the pradosh kala — the auspicious window that runs from sunset until roughly 2.5 hours after. It is one of the most accessible Shaiva observances, requiring only a lamp, some bilva leaves, and sincere intention.

## In Melbourne

The engine computes both the Trayodashi boundary and Melbourne's sunset time for AEDT to determine the pradosh window on any given day. Melbourne's spring and summer sunsets run late — past 8 pm AEDT in midsummer — which gives a generous pradosh window. When Trayodashi falls on a Saturday, the day is called Shani Pradosh and considered especially potent.

## The story

**Short version.** The gods and demons churned the cosmic ocean, releasing a poison (Halahala) that threatened all creation. Shiva swallowed the poison to save the worlds, and it lodged in his throat, turning it blue — hence one of his names, Nilakantha (blue-throated). The gods, relieved, praised Shiva at dusk. That evening praise became the origin of Pradosh worship.

**Full version.** The Skanda Purana and Shiva Purana describe the churning of the ocean (Samudra Manthan) in which among the things that arose was a terrible poison, Halahala, that could destroy all existence. The gods and demons were paralysed with fear. Shiva, acting without hesitation, drank the poison to protect creation, holding it in his throat at Parvati's request (she pressed his throat to stop it descending further). At the twilight hour after this act, the gods gathered and praised Shiva with great devotion. This evening twilight — pradosh kala — is therefore the time most beloved by Shiva, when he is understood to be most accessible to prayer. Pradosh Vrat formalises this into a twice-monthly observance. The Skanda Purana lists 16 kinds of Pradosh, each corresponding to the day of the week on which Trayodashi falls, with different boons attributed to each.

*Sources: Skanda Purana (Kartika Mahatmya), Shiva Purana*

## What to do

### Ten-minute version

1. At sunset, light a lamp at the home shrine (or before a Shiva murti or lingam)
2. Offer bilva (bel) leaves if available, or any flower
3. Recite "Om Namah Shivaya" for a few minutes
4. Remain with the lamp until it has burned for at least 20–30 minutes

### Fuller version

1. Fast through the day — most observers take only one meal before sunset, or abstain from grains; some do a full fast
2. Bathe before the evening puja
3. Set up the puja area with a Shiva lingam or image of Shiva and Parvati
4. Light the lamp at or just before sunset — the pradosh kala has begun
5. Offer bilva leaves (three-lobed; each leaf is considered equivalent to three days' worship), white flowers, milk, water, and vibhuti (sacred ash)
6. Pour water or raw milk over the lingam if you have one
7. Recite the Shiva Panchakshara Stotra ("Nagendra haraya…") or simply chant "Om Namah Shivaya" 108 times
8. Read or listen to the Pradosh Vrat Katha (story of the vrat) if available
9. Perform arati with camphor or a ghee lamp
10. Continue worship or sitting in the shrine space until approximately 2.5 hours after sunset; the fast may be broken with fruit or a light meal after this

### Items needed

- Oil lamp (diya) with ghee or sesame oil preferred
- Bilva (bel) leaves — three-lobed; very traditional for Shiva puja
- White flowers (dhatura, white lotus, jasmine — all traditional; any white flower works)
- Vibhuti (sacred ash) — applied to the forehead after puja
- Water or raw milk for abhisheka (optional but traditional)
- Incense

### In Melbourne

Bilva trees do not grow in Melbourne; bilva leaves are sometimes available dried at Indian grocers in Dandenong or Footscray during Shravan and festival seasons. Fresh curry leaves or tulsi are not substitutes in the strict sense, but any flower offered with intention is accepted. The Hindu Temple of Melbourne carries vibhuti. The pradosh window end-time is shown in the app alongside the sunset for each date.

## Why

**Scriptural.** The Skanda Purana states that worship during the pradosh kala on Trayodashi gives the merit of all tirthas (pilgrimages) and all sacrifices combined. The Shiva Purana identifies this as the moment Shiva's grace is most freely available.

**Symbolic.** Shiva's swallowing of the poison is an act of pure selflessness — he harmed himself to preserve all others. Worshipping at this twilight hour connects the devotee to that act: standing at the threshold between day and night, light and dark, in gratitude for protection that cost the protector everything.

**Temporal.** Pradosh literally means "dusk" — the liminal hour. Many Hindu traditions assign special spiritual potency to boundaries: the junction of night and day (Brahma Muhurta), of lunar fortnights (Amavasya, Purnima), and of tithis. Pradosh is the daily-level liminal moment captured in a fortnightly observance.

## How others do it

- **South India (especially Tamil Nadu):** Pradosh is observed with great regularity; Shiva temples hold special abhisheka (ritual bathing of the lingam) during the pradosh window; the temple circuit is walked three times during the window
- **North India:** Observation varies by family; Shani Pradosh (Saturday Trayodashi) draws large numbers to temples
- **Maharashtra:** Somawar (Monday) Pradosh is considered especially potent, as Monday is already Shiva's day
- **ISKCON and Vaishnavas:** Generally do not observe Pradosh — their fortnightly observance is Ekadashi

## Who is traditionally exempt

The full day's fast is optional — the pradosh kala worship is the non-negotiable core. Those who are unwell, elderly, pregnant, or nursing are not expected to fast and may eat normally through the day, then observe the evening lamp and prayer. Children may participate in the evening puja without any fasting requirement. The tradition is clear that the worship itself, not the fasting, is primary.
