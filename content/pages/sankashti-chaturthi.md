# Sankashti Chaturthi

## In two sentences

Observed on the Chaturthi (fourth tithi) of the Krishna Paksha (waning fortnight) every month, Sankashti Chaturthi is dedicated to Ganesha, and the fast is traditionally broken only after the moon has risen and been sighted. Sankashti means "relief from troubles," and the observance is understood as a way of asking Ganesha — the remover of obstacles — to clear the path ahead.

## The story

**Short version.** The moon once mocked Ganesha for his rotund form and large belly, and Ganesha cursed the moon to wane and diminish. The gods intervened, and the curse was softened — the moon would wax and wane in cycles, and on Chaturthi of the waning fortnight, devotees would sight the moon as an act of reconciliation and gratitude.

**Full version.** The Ganesha Purana and Shiva Purana contain versions of this story. On the night of Chaturthi of Bhadrapada Shukla Paksha (Ganesh Chaturthi), the moon laughed at Ganesha, who had overindulged in sweets and fallen from his mount. Ganesha, insulted, cursed the moon: those who see the moon on that night will be falsely accused. Over time, the Chaturthi of the waning fortnight became the monthly occasion to propitiate Ganesha and, by extension, to sight the now-humbled moon. The stories recited on Sankashti — called the Sankashti Vrat Katha — vary by month and by region, each narrating a different devotee's obstacles being removed by Ganesha's grace. The core theme across all of them is that Ganesha responds to sincere devotion by clearing the way for what matters — not by removing all difficulty, but by granting the capacity to move through it.

*Sources: Ganesha Purana, Shiva Purana, Mudgala Purana*

## What to do

### Ten-minute version

1. Fast through the day — or at minimum skip the midday meal; fruit is permitted
2. In the evening, light a lamp at the shrine and offer a flower or a modak (or any sweet) to Ganesha
3. After moonrise (check the app for Melbourne's moonrise time), sight the moon or look in its direction
4. Break the fast with a proper meal

### Fuller version

1. Wake early and bathe; intend the fast for the day
2. Observe a grain-free fast — fruits, milk, and permitted snacks are allowed; some families fast completely (nirjala) until moonrise
3. In the evening, set up the puja area with a Ganesha murti or image
4. Offer durva grass (three-bladed) — the most sacred offering for Ganesha; 21 blades of durva is traditional
5. Offer a modak or any sweet; coconut and jaggery are simple traditional alternatives
6. Light incense and a lamp; apply kumkum and sindoor to the murti
7. Recite the Ganesha Atharvashirsha (a short Upanishadic hymn to Ganesha), or the Ganesha Ashtottara (108 names), or simply chant "Om Gam Ganapataye Namah" 108 times
8. Read or listen to the Sankashti Vrat Katha for the specific month if available
9. After the puja, wait for moonrise (the app shows this); sight the moon, offer water to the moon (Arghya) while facing it, and thank Ganesha
10. Break the fast with a proper meal — traditionally the meal includes the sweets offered as prasad

### Items needed

- Durva grass (three-bladed) — 21 blades traditional
- Modak or any sweet (coconut and jaggery laddoo works well)
- Kumkum and sindoor
- Oil lamp and incense
- Ganesha murti or image
- Water in a small vessel for Arghya to the moon

## Why

**Scriptural.** The Ganesha Purana identifies the Krishna Chaturthi as the most auspicious time to propitiate Ganesha, because the waning moon represents diminishing obstacles. The specific merit of Sankashti is described as freedom from the obstacles (sankashti) of the coming month.

**Symbolic.** Ganesha sits at every threshold and beginning. A monthly observance that resets the relationship with the remover of obstacles is practically oriented — not cosmological or annual, but as regular as the month itself. The moonrise sighting ties the observance to an actual astronomical event, making it impossible to simply set a clock alarm; you must watch.

**Psychological.** The act of fasting through the day and then watching for the moon — which cannot be hurried — is a built-in practice of patience and attention. Sankashti is unusual among Hindu fasts because the break condition is natural and unscheduled: you wait until the moon appears.

## How others do it

- **Maharashtra:** Sankashti is extremely prominent; Angaraki Chaturthi (when it falls on Tuesday) sees large crowds at Siddhivinayak temple in Mumbai
- **Tamil Nadu:** Called Sankata Hara Chaturthi; observed similarly with moonrise sighting; particularly prominent among Tamil Brahmin families
- **Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh:** Observed as a regular monthly fast with similar structure; regional variations in which Katha is recited
- **North India:** Observed but less prominently than in Maharashtra and South India; the Ganesh Chaturthi of Bhadrapada (waxing fortnight) draws more attention in these regions
- **Vinayaka Chaturthi:** The Shukla Paksha Chaturthi (waxing fortnight) is called Vinayaka Chaturthi and is also auspicious for Ganesha — distinct from Sankashti, which is always on the waning fortnight

## Who is traditionally exempt

Children, the elderly, pregnant and nursing women, and those who are ill are exempt from the full fast and may eat normally. A partial fast — skipping one meal, or eating only fruit — carries partial merit and is always acceptable. The moonrise sighting and a short prayer to Ganesha are the irreducible minimum: the fast is the ideal, not the obligation.
