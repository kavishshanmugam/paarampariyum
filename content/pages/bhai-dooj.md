# Bhai Dooj

## In two sentences

The fifth and final day of Diwali, when sisters apply a tilak to their brothers' foreheads, pray for their long life, and feed them sweets — and brothers offer gifts in return. The festival draws on the ancient story of Yama, the god of death, visiting his sister Yamuna, who was so devoted that he granted a boon to all brothers who receive their sister's tilak on this day.

## The story

**Short version.** Yama, the god of death, visited his sister Yamuna on this day. Moved by her devotion, he declared that any brother who receives his sister's tilak on this tithi will be protected from untimely death. Yamuna is also identified with the Yamuna river.

**Full version.** The story is told in the Skanda Purana and referenced in the Mahabharata. Yama and Yamuna (also called Yami) are twin children of Surya, the sun god. Yamuna had long invited her brother to her home, and on Kartika Shukla Dwitiya, Yama finally came. She bathed him, anointed his forehead with tilak, fed him his favourite sweets, and prayed for his welfare. Yama, deeply moved — for he is rarely shown such tender care, being the god of death — granted her a boon: that any brother who is similarly honoured by his sister on this day will be freed from the fear of Yama. He also declared the day Yam Dwitiya ("Yama's second"). The story is also told in the context of Krishna: after killing Narakasura, Krishna visited his sister Subhadra, who welcomed him with a tilak and sweets. The two versions coexist across different regional traditions.

*Sources: Skanda Purana, Mahabharata (Anushasana Parva)*

## What to do

### Ten-minute version

1. The sister applies a tilak (kumkum or sandalwood paste) to the brother's forehead
2. She performs a small arati with a lamp, circling it before him
3. She feeds him a sweet
4. The brother gives a gift — it may be small; the gesture is what matters

### Fuller version

1. The sister prepares the puja thali: kumkum, rice, a diya, sweets, and optionally a coconut or flowers
2. She seats her brother, applies tilak on his forehead, and places some akshat (rice) on his head
3. She performs arati — circling the lamp before him
4. She ties a thread (optional, less common than Raksha Bandhan's thread but practiced in some families)
5. She feeds him sweets by hand — ideally something she has made herself
6. She prays aloud or silently for his long life and wellbeing
7. The brother accepts the tilak with gratitude and gives a gift: money, jewellery, clothing, or anything meaningful
8. Both siblings eat together
9. For those connected remotely: perform the tilak over a video call; the sister may apply it to a photo of the brother, and he applies it to himself on screen

### Items needed

- Kumkum (vermilion) or sandalwood paste for tilak
- Akshat (unbroken rice, optionally coloured yellow with turmeric)
- Oil lamp (diya) and wick
- Sweets (home-made or bought — barfi, laddoo, halwa all traditional)
- Small puja thali (plate)
- Gift for the brother (optional but customary)

## Why

**Scriptural.** Yama's boon is recorded in the Skanda Purana: the sister's love and prayer are capable of softening even death. This is a striking theological statement — that human devotion and relationship have power in the face of the universal.

**Symbolic.** Where Raksha Bandhan emphasises the brother's protection of the sister, Bhai Dooj reverses the gaze: here the sister is the agent of protection, praying for the brother's life. The exchange of care, food, and gifts is a complete loop of mutual belonging.

**Seasonal.** Bhai Dooj closes the Diwali cycle. After five days of collective celebration — cleaning, light, worship, food, community — the final act is the most intimate: two siblings, a lamp, and a prayer.

## How others do it

- **Bengal:** Called Bhai Phota; the sister applies a paste of sandalwood, kohl, and paddy to the brother's forehead using a metal stylus; the ritual phrasing is "I draw the mark; Yama the god of death is my brother's enemy"
- **Maharashtra:** Called Bhau Beej; celebrated on the same day; closely follows the same structure
- **Nepal:** Called Bhai Tika; the most elaborate version — sisters apply a seven-colour tilak, offer petals and walnuts, and the ritual includes specific songs; celebrated as the final day of Tihar
- **South India:** Less prominently celebrated as a Diwali event; the brother-sister bond is honoured through other festivals

## Who is traditionally exempt

Sisters who have no living brother may perform the tilak for a cousin or a close male friend treated as a brother. Brothers who are ill or far away receive the tilak by proxy or by post (a practice common in Indian diaspora families). There is no exemption based on religious observance — this is a family celebration, not a fast.
